Cost Minimisation

Cost Curves and Supply

Marginal and average cost, the shutdown price p̂ and the supply curve S(w,p); why increasing returns to scale and price taking do not mix. With a homogeneous technology: AC/MC = k (lecture 3).

Lecture 2: one step and two step profit maximisation

One Step vs Two Steps

The profit surface over input bundles: its peak (one step) lies on the expansion path at the best output (two steps). D(w,p) = H(w,S(w,p)).

Lecture 3 · Properties of the firm's optimal behaviour

The Cost Function

C(w,q) as a function of input prices: concave because the firm substitutes (C4), Shephard's lemma (C5), scaling all prices (C3, H3) and the symmetric matrix of price effects (H4).

Lecture 3: one-step vs two-step approach · Lecture 4: (∗), (∗∗)

Substitution and Scale Effects

When w₁ rises, the demand for input 1 falls twice: along the isoquant (substitution) and down the expansion path as output falls (scale). The two always add up to the total.

Lecture 4 · Comparative statics: "what if" questions

Lecture 4: dq*/dp = 1/C_qq and (**)

What If? Comparative Statics

How output and input demand respond to prices. A fall in w₁ moves z₁ twice, substitution then scale, so ordinary demand is flatter than conditional demand; the area left of H¹ is the change in cost.

Lecture 4: Marshall's law, (†)

Marshall's Law of Derived Demand

With constant returns, an industry's labour demand elasticity is a weighted average of the elasticity of substitution and the price elasticity of product demand, weighted by labour's cost share.

Lecture 4: translog, (&), (%), (#), ($)

Translog Cost Shares

The translog as a second-order approximation of a CES cost function, share equations linear in log prices, elasticities, the parameter restrictions, and the Arnberg and Bjørner (2007) results.

Lecture 4: Arnberg and Bjørner (2007)

Substitution or Composition?

Two Leontief firms that cannot substitute at all still make aggregate data look as if energy was replaced by capital: why the study uses variation within firms.

Lecture 5 · The firm and the market

Lecture 5: more firms

From Firms to Market Supply

Add the firms' supply curves. Fixed costs make supply jump, so demand can miss it: no equilibrium. With many identical firms the average firm's supply fills the gap.

Lecture 5: interaction between firms

Firms That Affect Each Other

When one firm's output raises the other's marginal cost, market supply is steeper than MC₁ + MC₂; when it lowers it, flatter.

Lecture 5: industry size

Free Entry and Industry Size

Profits attract entrants, the price falls, and entry stops when Π(q_N) ≥ 0 but Π(q_N+1) < 0. In the long run, zero profit at minimum average cost.

Lecture 5: market power, product differentiation

Monopoly and Product Differentiation

MR = MC and p = MC/(1 + 1/η): less output at a higher price. Substitutes push a local monopolist's average revenue down until it is tangent to average cost.

Lecture 5 · Consumer preferences

Lecture 5: consumption opportunities

Budget Sets

Income (B1), endowment (B2), both (B3) and a two-part tariff. A price change turns the budget line about different points; fees and whole units make the set non-convex.

Lecture 5: axioms on preferences

Better, Worse, Indifferent

B(x), W(x) and I(x) for five preference relations, which axioms each satisfies, and why lexicographic preferences are not continuous.

Lecture 5: utility, quasi-concavity, MRS

Utility Is Ordinal

U and f(U) rank bundles the same way and share their indifference curves. Quasi-concavity and Theorem 1 along the tangent; MRS₂₁ = U₁/U₂.

Lecture 6 · Demand theory

Lecture 6: (UMP) and (EMP), duality, Roy and Shephard

UMP and EMP: Two Sides of One Tangency

Maximising utility on a budget and minimising spending for a utility level pick the same bundle: D(p,y) = H(p,V(p,y)), Roy's identity and Shephard's lemma.

Lecture 6: (M3) the Slutsky equation, Giffen goods

Substitution and Income Effects

A price change splits into a move along the indifference curve and a parallel shift of the budget line. Marshallian against Hicksian demand, and a Giffen good whose demand slopes up.

Lecture 6: income effects, elasticities, Engel and Cournot

Income Expansion Paths and Engel Curves

How demand moves with income: necessities, luxuries and inferior goods, budget shares, and the conditions of Engel and Cournot.

Lecture 7 · Welfare measurement

Lecture 7: CV, EV and consumer surplus

CV, EV and Consumer Surplus

Three money measures of a price change as areas to the left of demand curves. The income effect decides their order; without it they coincide.

Lecture 7: the deadweight loss of a tax

The Deadweight Loss of a Tax

The consumer loses more than the tax raises. The loss grows with the square of the tax, and the notes' table of 14 commodity groups shows where each krone of tax costs most.

Lecture 8 · Decentralisation in a simple economy

Lecture 8: planner, prices and the (C1), (C2) conditions

Robinson Crusoe's Economy

Robinson's best mix of leisure and coconuts, MRS = MRT, and the real wage that lets a profit-maximising firm and a utility-maximising consumer find it on their own. Why it fails with increasing returns, and what trade does.

Lecture 9: offer curves, core, Walras' law, welfare theorems

The Edgeworth Box

Trade between Alf and Bill: offer curves, one or three competitive equilibria, the contract curve and the core, excess demand, and any efficient allocation reached by a lump-sum transfer.
